Part 2 script
“Welcome to Riverside Photography Club”
Good evening everyone, and welcome to your first session with Riverside Photography Club. I'm Dan, and I'll be running most of the Tuesday evening sessions this term.
Just a bit of background: the club was founded in nineteen ninety-four, which actually makes us one of the oldest photography clubs in the whole region. We meet here at the community centre every week, except during the month of August, when we take a short summer break.
This term, we're focusing mainly on portrait photography, which I know a few of you specifically signed up for. We'll spend the first four weeks on lighting techniques before moving on to editing software in the second half of term.
If you haven't brought a camera tonight, don't worry — we do have a small number of cameras you can borrow for the evening, though I'd recommend bringing your own from next week if you can, even if it's just the one on your phone.
Membership for the full term costs thirty-five pounds, and that includes access to our online resource library as well as the two field trips we run each term. Our first field trip this term will be to Alderley Castle, which most of you will know is about twenty minutes from here by car.
One thing I do need from everyone tonight is a completed registration form — you'll find these on the table by the door, and I'll need them back to me by next Tuesday at the very latest, so please don't forget.
Finally, a quick note on our online group: we post weekly challenges and feedback there, and it's run through an app called Lensview rather than the usual social media platforms, mainly because it lets us share full-resolution images without them losing quality.
Right, let's get started with tonight's session, which is going to be all about understanding your camera's manual settings.

Part 4 script

Interviewer: Today I'm talking to wildlife photographer Claire Byrne about her new book, out this month. Claire, welcome.

Claire: Thanks for having me.

Interviewer: So, this book focuses on urban wildlife specifically — animals living in cities. What drew you to that, after years photographing more remote locations?

Claire: It was almost an accident. I was between bigger expeditions, stuck in London for a few months longer than planned, and I started noticing foxes on my evening walks. I thought, well, I've got the equipment sitting here doing nothing, I may as well use it. What started as something to fill the time completely took over — I ended up spending eighteen months on it in the end.

Interviewer: And was it harder or easier than photographing wildlife somewhere like the Amazon, say?

Claire: People always assume it must be easier, because you're not dealing with extreme heat or difficult terrain, but I'd say it was harder in its own way. Urban animals are very wary of people — more wary than animals that rarely see humans at all — so you actually need more patience, not less. I spent whole nights getting nothing usable at all.

Interviewer: Was there a particular image in the book that mattered most to you?

Claire: There's a shot of a fox family denning under an old railway bridge that I'm proud of, but it's not actually my favourite in the book. My favourite is a much quieter image — just a heron standing completely still on a car park roof at dawn. It's not dramatic at all, but something about it captures exactly what I was trying to say with the whole project.

Interviewer: Which is?

Claire: That wildlife isn't only out there somewhere far away that most of us will never see. It's already here, right alongside us, if we're willing to actually look for it.

Interviewer: Do you have advice for someone wanting to get into this kind of photography themselves?

Claire: Don't wait for the perfect equipment — that's probably my main advice. People always ask me about camera gear and think that's the barrier, but the biggest difference is just spending time outdoors, learning the patterns of the animals near you. I made most of my best early images with equipment I'd now consider fairly basic.

Interviewer: And what's next for you?

Claire: I'm actually taking a proper break for the first time in years. It feels strange to admit that. After that, I have a vague idea about a similar project somewhere in Spain, maybe around Valencia — there's a lot of birdlife in the wetlands just south of the city that hasn't really been documented. But that's still just an idea at this stage, nothing confirmed.

Interviewer: Claire Byrne, thank you very much.

Claire: Thank you.
